Just-in-Time Write Access to Azure with PIM

7 min read · 21 September 2026

Engineers need to see everything and change very little. Most days they read metrics, check configuration and look at logs. A few times a week they actually need to change something. Yet the common setup gives them Contributor on every subscription, all day, every day, because that's the easiest way to avoid tickets.

The pattern I prefer is simple: everyone gets Reader permanently, and write access is something you switch on for a few hours with Microsoft Entra Privileged Identity Management (PIM). Here's how I set it up across dev, test, staging and production, and the gaps you need to know about.

The shape of it

  • Standing access: Reader on the subscriptions or resource groups the team supports. Enough to troubleshoot, not enough to break anything.
  • Eligible access: a write role, assigned as eligible in PIM rather than active. Engineers activate it when they need it.
  • The narrowest role that does the job. If the team only restarts web apps, give them Website Contributor, not Contributor. Owner should almost never be eligible for engineers.

What activation looks like

An engineer opens PIM in the Microsoft Entra admin center, goes to My roles, finds the eligible Azure resource role and selects Activate. Depending on how you configure the role, they then:

  1. Prove who they are, either with MFA or with a Conditional Access authentication context (more on that below).
  2. Enter a justification and a ticket number.
  3. Optionally narrow the scope. If they're eligible on a subscription, they can activate on just one resource group.
  4. Wait for approval, if the role requires it.

Once approved, the active assignment appears within seconds and disappears when the time runs out or the engineer deactivates early. Some tools cache tokens, so a sign-out and sign-in can be needed before new or removed permissions show up.

Settings per environment

Role settings in PIM are defined per role and per resource, and they don't inherit. Settings on a subscription don't flow down to its resource groups. That's the detail that catches people out: if you make someone eligible at a resource group but only configured settings at the subscription, the resource group uses its own defaults. Configure settings at every scope where you create eligible assignments, ideally with code.

The activation limit can be anywhere from 1 to 24 hours. Shorter isn't always safer. If production work routinely takes three hours and the limit is one, people activate repeatedly and approvers start rubber-stamping. Pick a window that fits real work.

For approvals, use a group of at least two people. Approvers don't need any Azure role themselves, which is handy: a lead can approve without holding write access.

Why I use an authentication context instead of plain MFA

The plain "require MFA" setting has a weakness. If the user already satisfied MFA earlier in the session, PIM may not ask again. For staging and production I want a fresh, strong check at the moment of activation.

So I use On activation, require Microsoft Entra Conditional Access authentication context and point it at a Conditional Access policy that:

  • requires a phishing-resistant authentication strength, and
  • sets sign-in frequency to Every time, so the user reauthenticates on every activation.

Create and enable that policy before you reference the context in PIM. If no policy targets the context, PIM falls back to plain MFA, but only in that one case. The fallback doesn't kick in if the policy is turned off, left in report-only mode, or excludes the user. In those cases, activation goes ahead without the extra check. It also means whoever manages Conditional Access effectively controls your production access, so treat those admins as highly privileged.

The gap people miss

Conditional Access checks at activation only apply to the activation. Once the role is active, nothing stops the user from signing in on a different, unmanaged device and using the permission there.

If device compliance matters to you, and for production it should, also apply a Conditional Access policy that requires a compliant device for the eligible users themselves, all the time, not just during activation.

Evidence: who was allowed, and what they did

Two logs, two questions:

  • PIM audit history answers "who was allowed to do this, when, and why?" It records eligibility, requests, approvals, activations and expiry, including the justification and ticket number.
  • Azure Activity Log answers "what changed?" It records control-plane write operations against resources.

Joining them by user and time is the useful part. Every write in the Activity Log from an engineer should fall inside one of their activation windows. A write outside any window means someone still has standing access you didn't know about. That single check finds more problems than any access review I've run.

The Activity Log keeps 90 days by default. Send it to a Log Analytics workspace if you want to query it alongside PIM data or keep it longer.

The cost

This does slow people down. An activation that expires mid-task means another request, and an approver who's asleep can block an incident fix. Plan for that: make sure the approver group covers your on-call hours, and keep a break-glass process for real emergencies that's tested and monitored, not a standing Owner assignment someone forgot about.

Checklist

  • Engineers hold Reader only as standing access.
  • Write roles are eligible in PIM, never active, and as narrow as possible.
  • Role settings exist at every scope with eligible assignments.
  • Staging and production use an authentication context with "Every time" sign-in frequency.
  • A Conditional Access policy requires compliant devices for eligible users at all times.
  • Approver groups have at least two people and cover on-call hours.
  • Activity Log writes are regularly matched against PIM activation windows.
  • Eligible assignments are reviewed and expire, so people who changed teams lose them.
