Using Claude for Design Work, Not Just Code

⏱ 6 min read 📅 May 17, 2026 ✍️ Srinivas Yenuganti
Claude Architecture ADR Threat Modeling AI Assistants

The most useful thing I do with Claude isn't writing code. It's arguing with me about designs.

When I'm deciding how to structure a network, where to draw an identity boundary or which of two services to use, I want someone to poke holes in it before it's built. Colleagues are the best option, and they're busy. A model that has read a lot of architecture writing is a good second, as long as I'm clear about what it's for. This post is how I use it, and the rules I follow about what goes into a prompt.

Give it the whole problem, in writing

The quality of the answer tracks the quality of the context. I write a short brief before asking anything:

Context: multi-tenant SaaS API on Azure, about 40 customers,
each with its own database. Regulated data. Two regions.

Constraints: no public endpoints on data services; customer
data must stay in-region; the team is five engineers who
already run App Service and Azure SQL well.

Current design: [two or three paragraphs]

Options I'm weighing:
A) one App Service plan per customer
B) shared App Service plans, isolation at the data layer

Question: which would you choose for this team and why?
Then argue for the other one as strongly as you can.

Writing the brief is half the value. I often spot the answer myself while doing it.

Ask it to argue against you

Models tend to agree with whoever's asking. Counter that on purpose. My favorite prompts:

  • "Argue against my chosen option as if you'd have to run it in production."
  • "What are the three most likely ways this fails in the first year?"
  • "What happens at ten times the customers? At one tenth of the team?"
  • "Which part of this design is hardest to reverse?"

Most of what comes back I've already considered. The one point I hadn't is the reason to do it.

Let it draft the boring parts

Architecture work produces documents that matter and that nobody enjoys writing. A model is good at first drafts of them:

  • Architecture decision records. Microsoft's Well-Architected guidance suggests every record include the problem and context, the options considered, the decision with its trade-offs and your confidence level, and a status such as Proposed, Accepted or Superseded. It also recommends treating the log as append-only: when a decision changes, write a new record that supersedes the old one. I give the model my notes and that template, and edit what it produces.
  • Threat model tables. Given a data flow, it can produce a first pass of threats per component using STRIDE. I then delete the generic ones and add the specific ones it missed.
  • Runbook skeletons. Preconditions, steps, how to verify, how to roll back. The real commands come from me and get tested.

Have it check your reasoning

After I've written a decision, I paste it back and ask: "What did I assume without saying so? What fact, if it turned out differently, would change this decision?" That second question is the one I use most. It turns hidden assumptions into things I can go and check.

What I don't use it for

  • Facts I won't verify. Azure changes constantly, and services get renamed. Azure AI Foundry is Microsoft Foundry now, for example. Anything about limits, SKUs, regions or feature support gets checked against Microsoft Learn before it goes in a design.
  • Final security or compliance calls. It can help me think about a control. Whether the control satisfies a regulation is a call for people who own that risk.
  • Making the decision. It has no stake in the outcome and won't be there when it goes wrong.

What never goes in a prompt

The rules are simple and I don't bend them:

  • No secrets, keys, connection strings or tokens. Not even "just this once."
  • No customer data and no PHI. If I need an example record, I describe the schema and ask for synthetic data.
  • No real hostnames, IP ranges, tenant or subscription IDs. Placeholders work just as well for design work.
  • Only through an access path my organization has approved. Claude models can be deployed in Microsoft Foundry, for example, which keeps usage inside an Azure resource you control. Check which terms apply to the specific model and deployment type you use.

A 30-minute design review routine

  1. Write the brief: context, constraints, current design, options. (10 minutes)
  2. Ask for a recommendation and the strongest case against it. (5 minutes)
  3. Ask for the top failure modes and the hardest-to-reverse decision. (5 minutes)
  4. Write my decision in an ADR draft and ask what I assumed. (10 minutes)
  5. Take the open questions to a colleague, a prototype or the docs.

Step 5 is where the real work happens. The model's job is to make sure I walk into it with better questions.
