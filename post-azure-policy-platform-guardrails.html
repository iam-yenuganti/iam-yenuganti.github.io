Moving Azure Policy from Audit to Enforcement

7 min read · 20 September 2026

Every platform team I've worked with has a pile of Azure Policy assignments in audit mode. The compliance dashboard is red, everyone agrees it should be green, and nobody wants to be the person who flips a policy to deny and breaks a release on a Friday.

Moving from audit to enforcement doesn't have to be a leap. Here's how I do it in small, reversible steps.

Group policies into initiatives

Assigning forty individual policies to a management group is forty things to track. I group related rules into initiatives (policy sets): one for networking, one for identity and keys, one for logging, one for tagging and cost. Each initiative gets assigned once, high in the management group tree, and the compliance view rolls up per initiative so you can see which area is behind.

Keep each initiative small enough that one team owns it. When an initiative mixes networking, tagging and encryption, nobody feels responsible for its compliance number.

Keep everything in code

Definitions, initiatives, assignments and exemptions all live in a repository and deploy through a pipeline. Changes go through a pull request, so there's a record of who changed a guardrail and why. The portal is for looking at results, not for editing policy.

Roll out with resource selectors

The feature that made enforcement much less scary for me is resource selectors on the assignment. They limit which resources the assignment evaluates, so you can start small and widen the net by editing one property. A selector can match on three things:

  • resourceLocation: the region a resource lives in.
  • resourceType: for example, only storage accounts.
  • resourceWithoutLocation: subscription-level resources that have no region.

Microsoft's safe deployment guidance uses region as the rollout ring, which works well if your non-production workloads live in a different region from production. If they don't, use separate assignments per environment's management group instead.

A first-stage assignment looks like this:

"enforcementMode": "DoNotEnforce",
"resourceSelectors": [{
  "name": "rollout-ring",
  "selectors": [{
    "kind": "resourceLocation",
    "in": [ "westus3" ]
  }]
}]

DoNotEnforce means the policy evaluates and reports, but doesn't block or change anything. That's how you find out what would break.

The sequence I follow

  1. Assign in DoNotEnforce, first ring only. Wait for the compliance scan and read the results. Surprises here are cheap.
  2. Fix or exempt what you find. Some resources need changing. Others have a real reason to be different, and they get an exemption.
  3. Switch the first ring to Default enforcement. Run tests that try to create a non-compliant resource and confirm it's blocked.
  4. Widen the selector. Add the next region or environment, check compliance and application health, then continue.
  5. Remove the selector once enforcement covers everything.

Each step is one small change to the assignment, and each can be undone the same way.

Exemptions, done properly

An exemption excludes a specific scope or resource from an assignment without weakening the policy for everyone else. There are two categories, and the difference matters in an audit:

  • Mitigated: the intent is met another way. A storage account without a private endpoint that sits behind a network control you can show the auditor.
  • Waiver: you're knowingly accepting the risk for now.

Every exemption I create has three things: an expiry date, metadata pointing to the ticket and approver, and a named owner. Waivers without an expiry turn into permanent holes. Exemptions can use resource selectors too, which is handy when you need to exempt one resource type in one region.

Remediation for existing resources

deny only stops new problems. For resources that already exist, policies with deployIfNotExists or modify effects can fix them through remediation tasks. A diagnostic settings policy that sends logs to your workspace is the classic example.

Remediation runs as the assignment's managed identity, so that identity needs the right roles. List exactly those roles in the policy definition's roleDefinitionIds. The portal assigns them for you; if you deploy with templates or the CLI, you create the role assignments yourself, and forgetting this is the usual reason a remediation task fails.

Remediation tasks aren't continuous. New non-compliant resources are handled at deployment time by the policy effect, but anything that drifted needs a new task. I trigger them from the pipeline after each policy change.

Things that go wrong

  • A deny blocks a platform deployment because the platform's own resources weren't considered. Run your landing zone deployment against the first ring before enforcing.
  • Remediation can't write because the managed identity lacks a role at the right scope.
  • Exemptions pile up and nobody reviews them. Report on expiring and expired exemptions every month.

Checklist

  • Policies grouped into small initiatives with clear owners.
  • Definitions, assignments and exemptions deployed from code.
  • New assignments start in DoNotEnforce with a narrow resource selector.
  • Enforcement widened ring by ring, with tests that prove the policy blocks what it should.
  • Every exemption has a category, expiry, ticket and owner.
  • Remediation identities have only the roles in roleDefinitionIds.
